Library V3 destinations — take 3, from the research

Reaching Liked Songs, Albums, Artists, Podcasts and Local files from the Library V3 pane (issue #85 H4). Counts are warm (LibraryStats), routes are registered (ShellRoutes), the pane resizes 180–460 DIP and collapses to a 56-DIP rail. The question is only what it should look like — and this take answers it from what shipped apps do and what their users actually say, not from taste. Evidence first, then three shapes.

✗ Rejected (take 1, shipped): five full-width rows above “Your Library”. ~200 DIP of chrome, reads as Classic bolted onto V3, pushes the library below the fold.

✗ Currently implemented (take 2, option A): five equal 52-DIP icon tiles whose 10 px labels drop entirely below ~270 DIP of pane (LibraryV3Metrics.DestinationLabelW). The research is unkind to it on three counts: icon-only navigation is the best-documented navigation failure mode there is; no shipped desktop music app puts a tile strip inside a persistent sidebar; and five equal tiles assert that Liked Songs and Local files matter equally, which no usage pattern supports and which Microsoft’s own navigation guidance explicitly argues against.

⚠ Two bugs the research trip turned up in passing. (1) Liked Songs renders twice in the expanded pane today — as a destination tile (LibraryV3NavBand.DestinationStrip, unconditional) and as a list row (LibraryV3Document’s v3.liked, suppressed only when Liked is in the top bar, which the tile strip is not). (2) The 56-DIP rail shows none of the five — LibraryV3Sidebar.BuildRailHead draws prefs.TopBar only. All three proposals fix both.

What the research says

What users complain about

  • “Cluttered sidebar” is the literal phrase. On Spotify’s 2023 “Your Library” sidebar: the library is squished uncomfortably to a cluttered sidebar, and this UI is so cluttered yet it’s designed in such a way that you’re only looking at one thing at a time. community.spotify.com/…/5571384 · …/5525566 (16+ pages)
  • Chrome that eats the pane is what people hate most — not the number of destinations: Why should I give up half of my desktop size to have an overview of my albums and artists? I think it’s a huge downgrade and makes my browsing experience much worse. windowslatest.com
  • Losing the dedicated Albums/Artists entries is the loudest single loss. An entire thread titled “‘Your Library’ redesign, where are my artists / albums?”, including The library redesign is the main reason why I switched to Apple Music. community.spotify.com/…/4676709 · hypebot · inverse
  • The filter system itself is named as the regression. On HN: the UX got particularly bad when they introduced the filter-system; and When I open Spotify, I usually know what I want to listen to. I strongly suspect the redesign is going to make it so that it takes me more time to get there. news.ycombinator.com/item?id=35122439
  • People want a filter to behave like a place. “Pin a filter type to make it the default” has been an open request for years — the clearest evidence that the filter/destination distinction is a burden users would rather not carry. community.spotify.com/…/5198188
  • Click cost gets counted out loud: It takes 7 clicks in the app to get to them (their own playlists). community.spotify.com/…/7456630
  • Hiding nav behind a disclosure is the one arrangement with universal backlash. iTunes 11/12 hid the Library + Playlists sidebar by default: “less intuitive” (Macworld), “Apple’s worst software ever” (Mac Observer), “Apple’s quiet war on the sidebar” (Alphr). This retires take-2’s option B (destinations behind a header caret) for good. macworld · macobserver · alphr
  • Moving library nav out of the sidebar is remembered as “broke muscle memory”. Plex reverted to sidebar navigation after more than a year of complaints, and its pin mechanism has its own “FIX the sidebar Library pins pleeeeeeease!” thread. howtogeek · forums.plex.tv · xda
  • Icon-only navigation is the best-documented failure mode in the field. NN/g: Due to the absence of a standard usage for most icons, text labels are necessary to communicate the meaning and reduce ambiguity; Icon labels should be visible at all times, without any interaction from the user; Don’t rely on hover to reveal text labels: not only does it increase the interaction cost, but it also fails to translate well on touch devices. Only home, print and the search magnifier have mostly universal recognition — “albums”, “artists”, “shows”, “local files” do not. nngroup.com/articles/icon-usability
  • Users are more sensitive to churn than to the arrangement chosen. YouTube Music’s library and playlist redesign drew an “uproar” with users asking for a way back. androidpolice
  • Power users mourn density, not decoration. Apple Music’s loss of the iTunes Column Browser: With thousands of albums scrolling through pretty pictures or an endless song list is not practical. developer.apple.com/forums/thread/117308

What the guidance and the good examples do

  • Microsoft’s answer to this exact question is a horizontal, text-labelled strip. WinUI NavigationView: We recommend top navigation when: You have 5 or fewer top-level navigation categories that are equally important … You need to show all navigation options on screen. You want more space for your app content. Icons cannot clearly describe your app’s navigation categories. All four clauses describe this case: five destinations, all visible, the library needs the height, and albums-vs-artists-vs-shows is not an icon story. learn.microsoft.com/…/navigationview
  • Unequal treatment is explicitly endorsed. Navigation basics: Simplicity: Fewer navigation items simplify decision making for users. Providing easy access to important destinations and hiding less important items will help users get where they want, faster. Five equal tiles is the opposite of that sentence. learn.microsoft.com/…/navigation-basics
  • There is a sanctioned home for secondary destinations: the end of the pane. You can use FooterMenuItems to place navigation items at the end of the navigation pane, contrasted with the MenuItems property which places items at the start of the pane … Only Navigation items should be placed in FooterMenuItems. Windows 11’s own Media Player parks its pane-level affordance at the bottom of the nav bar. learn.microsoft.com/…/navigationview · thurrott
  • Counts belong on nav items as numerals — and must not be mixed shapes. We recommend that you not use different types of InfoBadges in one NavigationView. Four destinations with a count plate and one (Local files) without is precisely that mismatch, so the count has to read as an inline numeral rather than a badge. learn.microsoft.com/…/info-badge
  • Zune did word-nav, not icon-nav. Metro grew out of Zune and Media Center with more focus on clean typography and less on UI chrome (“content before chrome”); Zune used large, bold and sans-serif fonts (mainly Segoe) … text often used as a primary navigation element and words being cut off at the edge of the screen to create a sense of movement. Its Collection screen was praised for exactly what V3 wants: Rather than them being separate section as in WMP, you can see Artists, Albums and individual songs all at once. wikipedia: Metro · davidvkimball · cloudywithachanceoflicensing
  • Zune’s home was Pins · New · History plus Smart DJ — a tiny, typographic fixed set, and a pin idiom Wavee already ships. gearlive · gsmarena
  • Metro’s own split between its two horizontal patterns maps onto navigate-vs-filter. A pivot is for more efficient, focused, and habitual usage, to apply data filters, change app views, and display related content over one dataset (≤7 items, the next header greyed out and peeking in from the edge); a panorama is for browsing distinct destinations. The greyed, clipped peek is the canonical way a horizontal strip says “there is more here”. timheuer · jeffwilcox: Metro design guide v1
  • The most durable, least-criticised idiom is a small fixed block the user can edit. Apple Music’s “Library” section ships ~5 rows (Recently Added, Artists, Albums, Songs) and keeps the other five behind Edit → check/uncheck → drag to reorder. Wavee already owns that machinery (the sidebar customizer). support.apple.com · macrumors
  • The second durable idiom is pin-to-top-of-the-same-list. Explorer’s Quick access above the tree has shipped for a decade with no backlash; Spotify’s 4 pins, Deezer’s new Quick Access and Plex’s pins are the same move. The fixed set becomes permanently-sorted-first members of the one list rather than separate chrome. digitalcitizen · alternativeto
  • The third is to move the fixed set out of the pane into a tab row in the content area — the new Windows 11 Media Player (Songs / Albums / Artists / Genres above the content) and Tidal (“My Collection” opens its own tab row). Not available here: the ask is reachability from the pane. geekchamp · tidal support
  • Chunking beats a longer flat scan. Hick’s law: reaction time grows with log₂(n) of the choices, and the standard mitigation is grouping — “twenty items in four groups of five is cognitively cheaper than a flat list of twenty”. One visually distinct destination band is one decision, not five. dovetail · nngroup (Hick’s law)
  • Research gap, reported honestly: no shipped desktop music app was found that puts a horizontal icon/tile strip for library destinations inside a persistent sidebar. Tile grids exist (Symfonium, Poweramp) but only as configurable home-screen dashboards; Slack’s icon rail is the closest precedent and it needed a second pane to work. The currently-implemented strip is therefore a departure from precedent, not an established idiom. docs.symfonium.app · slack.com/blog

Proposal 1 — the word rail recommended

A WinUI top-nav strip in Zune’s vocabulary: five words, no boxes, no fills, no pills — 13.5 px type on a 30-DIP band between Home and the “Your Library” rule, with a 2-DIP accent underline under the destination you are on and the next word clipped by a fade at the right edge. It is the one shape Microsoft’s own guidance names for “5 or fewer categories, all visible, and icons can’t describe them”, and the one Zune actually used.

Grammar. Two horizontal strips, deliberately opposite: the destinations are naked type with an underline above the rule; the filters are filled 12.5 px pills below the header. Different size, different weight, different shape, different side of the divider — nothing about a word row reads as a pill. One numeral on screen keeps the band calm.

Rail. The five become the leading tiles of RailHead above the existing divider — the delegate is already wired, it just draws TopBar today. Icon-only here is the one place NN/g’s objection does not bite: a 56-DIP rail is a state the user explicitly collapsed into, every tile has a tooltip, and the labelled form is one click away.

Verdict

✓ Every label always visible · 30 DIP · nothing hidden behind a caret · no precedent problem

What the research says they’ll like. The five names are legible at every pane width, which is the single thing NN/g is most emphatic about. It is the arrangement Microsoft names verbatim for this case (5 or fewer, all on screen, more space for content, icons can’t describe the categories). It costs 30 DIP rather than 200, which answers the loudest complaint in the corpus (why should I give up half of my desktop size). And it looks like Wavee’s stated ancestor: Zune navigated by words, not glyphs.

What they’ll complain about. (1) Two horizontal strips in one pane — someone will read the word row as “more filters” at first glance; the underline, the size step and the rule between them are the only defence, and they have to be drawn crisply. (2) Local files gets clipped at the narrow end, so it is peek-and-scroll rather than always visible. (3) No count except the active one — a user who liked seeing “18 albums” at a glance loses it (the numbers are on the pages, and hover restores them). (4) A word rail is not skimmable by icon, so returning users lose the shape-recognition that tiles would have given them.

Build. Replace LibraryV3NavBand.DestinationStrip with DestinationRail: a ScrollView (horizontal, no bar) of five text buttons, selection = a 2-DIP accent rectangle at the band’s bottom edge, roving focus with ←/→ exactly like LibraryV3ChipStrip’s. LibraryV3Metrics: DestinationTileH/CompactH/LabelW → one DestinationRailH = 30f. Delete the v3.liked section from LibraryV3Document (the rail owns Liked now, which also fixes the double-render) and drop the chips’ double-click-to-navigate affordance (V3ChipSlot.Route) — with a visible destination for each, a hidden gesture is redundant.

Free variant — the footer arm. The same control mounted at the bottom of the pane instead (WinUI’s FooterMenuItems convention: “navigation items at the end of the navigation pane”). Cost above the fold: zero — the library starts directly under the header. Needs one new SidebarPaneConfig.Foot delegate mirroring Head, plus RailFooter (already exists) for the collapsed form. Shown below.

Why it might be the better arm. It removes the last trace of the original complaint — nothing at all sits between the header and the first playlist — and it is the only option here with explicit first-party sanction for where it goes. Why it might not: a strip at the pane’s bottom edge sits directly above the now-playing bar, so the window’s bottom-left corner gets busy; and “at the bottom” is measurably less discoverable than “at the top” for a first-run user who has never scrolled the pane.

Proposal 2 — the pivot title

Zero new chrome. The static “Your Library” title is already 44 DIP of the biggest type in the pane; make it the destination switch. Library · Albums · Artists · Podcasts · Local at 16 px display type, the active word bright and underlined, the next word greyed and peeking past the fade — the literal Metro pivot. Picking a word both navigates the content area and scopes the pane’s list, which collapses the two verbs into one: the big type says where you are, the small pills say what you are looking at. Liked Songs stays what it already is — the first row of the list (v3.liked), art tile and all — because it is a playlist, not a category.

Verdict

~ Cheapest in pixels, boldest in behaviour, biggest in build cost

What they’ll like. It is the only option that removes controls instead of adding one: the pane loses a chip band most of the time and gains nothing. It answers the loudest structural complaint in the corpus head-on — “pin a filter type to make it the default”, i.e. people want a filter to be a place — by making it literally one thing. The biggest type in the pane always says where you are, which is Metro’s typographic hierarchy done properly. And 129 DIP of chrome is less than V3 spends today without any destinations at all.

What they’ll complain about. (1) Only ~3 words fit at 300 DIP and ~1.5 at 180, because the header also carries ▽ ＋ ⋯ ‹ — so the pivot is genuinely a scroller, and “Local” is always past the fade. (2) A title that scrolls is unusual; some users will not realise the words are tappable until they hover. (3) Local files scopes badly — local tracks are not library entries, so “the pane shows this destination’s contents” is a promise that arm cannot fully keep. (4) The churn risk the research is most consistent about: users forgive an arrangement, they do not forgive relearning one. This changes the meaning of the pane’s title and the meaning of a chip tap in one release.

Build. The real cost. LibraryV3Header’s title becomes a horizontal ScrollView pivot; LibraryV3ChipStrip.Facets shrinks to the qualifier grammar and LibraryV3Chrome must drop the chip band when Slots() is empty (a 40-DIP band that appears and disappears needs a layout transition); SidebarV3Filter and the persisted code become a destination rather than a filter, i.e. a migration; the ＋ folds into ⋯ below ~260 DIP to give the pivot room. Two to three days, not two hours.

Proposal 3 — quick access, inside the list

The most precedent-backed shape, and the most boring: the five destinations stop being chrome and become the first band of the list — Explorer’s Quick access above the tree, Spotify’s pins, Deezer’s Quick Access, Apple Music’s Library section. Compact 32-DIP rows under one small uppercase header, above the pin band, filter-aware (the Albums lens keeps Albums, drops Podcasts), scrollable away, and editable in the customizer exactly as Apple Music’s Edit does. Chrome above the list: unchanged.

The pane at 300 DIP

0 DIP of new chrome. The band is content: 26 + 5×32 = 186 DIP of list, which scrolls, filters and can be turned off — none of which the rejected version could do.

At 180 the rows just ellipsize — nothing sheds a label, nothing scrolls sideways, no peek to learn. It is the only one of the three that is completely unremarkable at the narrow floor, which is an argument in its favour.

Verdict

~ The safest shape on record and the one closest in appearance to what was rejected

What they’ll like. It is the arrangement with the longest unbroken track record — Explorer has shipped Quick access above a variable tree for a decade with no redesign backlash, and Apple Music’s editable Library section is the least-criticised music-app sidebar in the corpus. Labels are always full, counts are always visible, and it is the only option where the destinations obey the filter, which is what the “make my filter stick” requests keep asking for. Hick’s-law-correct too: one labelled group, one decision.

What they’ll complain about. (1) It looks like five stacked rows, which is the thing already rejected once — the differences (Compact 32 not Cozy 40, below the header not above it, scrolls, filters, hideable) are real but they are structural, and the first impression is visual. (2) It pushes the first playlist to ~315 DIP on a cold pane, even though a flick fixes it — and “I have to scroll past chrome to reach my playlists” is a documented complaint verbatim. (3) A second header (“Library” under “Your Library”) is two headers in one pane, which V3 removed on purpose (§3.2.7 renders no section headers).

Build. The cheapest of the three by far — it is the section V3 never mounted. LibraryV3Document.Build gains one SidebarSectionKind.CollectionShortcuts section (Compact density, count badges, a per-lens membership predicate) ahead of the pin band, and DestinationStrip is deleted from the nav band. Roughly the ten lines take-1 was, plus the lens predicate and the customizer’s hide/reorder — with the header the section already knows how to draw.

Recommendation: proposal 1, the word rail — top arm — and treat the footer arm as a one-line A/B.

It is the only shape here that no strand of the research argues against. Microsoft’s guidance names this exact configuration for this exact problem (“5 or fewer top-level categories … show all navigation options on screen … more space for your app content … icons cannot clearly describe your categories”). NN/g’s one non-negotiable — labels visible at all times, never behind hover — is satisfied at every pane width, which the implemented tile strip provably is not. It costs 30 DIP where take 1 cost 200, which is the complaint users voice most often and most vividly. And it is the shape Wavee’s own ancestor used: Zune navigated by words, in Segoe, clipped at the edge to say there was more — the same peek this rail uses at 180 DIP.

It is also the cheapest change with the largest cleanup attached: swapping the tile strip for a word rail lets the duplicated v3.liked row go, gives the 56-DIP rail its missing destinations through a delegate that already exists, and retires the chips’ undiscoverable double-click-to-navigate gesture. Net motion in the pane is 66 DIP back to the library.

If the word rail reads as “more filters” in the running app — the one risk the mock cannot settle — the fallback is proposal 3, not the tiles: it is the arrangement with the longest record of not annoying anyone, and it is a smaller diff than what is in the tree today. Proposal 2 is the destination, not the next commit: merging the destination switch into the pane’s title is the most coherent end state and the one that finally makes “filter” and “place” the same word, but it changes the meaning of the header and of a chip tap simultaneously, and the research is clearest of all on this: users forgive an arrangement, they do not forgive relearning one.
